This project asks: How much personal data do social media companies collect from users through their devices, and what are the privacy implications? I researched developer-reported privacy disclosures in Apple App Store privacy labels and compared them with reports from the Federal Trade Commission and Pew Research Center. I created an interactive website that allows visitors to compare ten major social media apps by the categories of data they say they may collect, whether their disclosures include tracking, and an educational “exposure score” based on the number and sensitivity of disclosed categories.

The comparison shows that most major platforms report collecting several kinds of personal information, commonly including identifiers, contact information, user content, usage data, and diagnostics. Many also disclose location, purchase, contact-list, search-history, or other potentially sensitive information. The project also shows an important limitation: app-store labels are supplied by developers, may vary by feature, age, and region, and do not measure how frequently data is collected or exactly how it is used.

I learned that staying connected involves a trade-off between convenience and privacy. Users often receive personalized feeds, communication tools, and free services, while companies gain detailed information that can support advertising, recommendations, analytics, and platform security. The most responsible response is not necessarily to stop using social media, but to understand permissions, review privacy settings, limit unnecessary access, and treat privacy labels as a starting point rather than a complete audit.
